# Chapter 3: Until Tomorrow

On the evening of February fifth I put two bowls on the desk, looked at them, and put one back in the drawer.

The second bowl had been for Fumi. There was no good reason for it. I was making instant noodles, which she would have called an insult to a kitchen, and the kitchen in question had been gone for almost sixty years even inside the simulation. I put the packet in the one remaining bowl and went to fill the kettle.

My left arm straightened when I reached for it. That still caught me sometimes.

The System waited until I had finished eating before it opened the invitation again.

[Begin heroine simulation: Raiden Mei? Yes / No]

I moved the panel out of the middle of my vision.

"You can stop doing that."

[You keep looking at the space where it was.]

"I work with interfaces. An open notification bothers me."

[Shall I put a little number in a red circle on it?]

I washed the bowl, dried it with a shirt that was already in the laundry, and put it away. At the end of the bed my running shoes were drying on newspaper. I had got through the last two days without a simulation, without throwing up, and without Asami asking whether I was on something. She had asked whether I was eating. I had shown her a banana.

The panel opened when I asked for it.

```
[Status]
Name: Eros
Age: 22
Combat Rating: Civilian
Honkai Adaptability: F
Soul: Frayed
Gifts: Quick Learner (Green), Sprinter (White), Quick Reflexes (Green)
Experience: Hunting and Field Craft (Professional)
Fate Fragments: 350
```

Three hundred and fifty. Enough to choose a normal life with fifty left over, assuming the choice was worth anything. I could ask for a safer place than Siberia and perhaps live long enough to get something useful out of it. I could also spend the next six hours as a very successful accountant and wake up with excellent bookkeeping and the same F.

The soul repair cost five hundred.

I opened the shop, looked at the number again in case it had become smaller, and closed it.

"Can the heroine run offer an Adaptability raise?"

[It can.]

"Can you guarantee one?"

[No. I can give you a chance at one.]

"And if I do well?"

[A better chance.]

"You're very careful with that word."

[You're paying attention. Good.]

I leaned back against the wall. On my phone the Nagazora alert was still in the notification bar. The last spike had come at four that afternoon while I was explaining to Mori why the simulation room could not use the live weather feed to decide whether a floor was slippery. He had said it was a reasonable request from the training staff. I had asked whether he wanted to be the one to explain why a girl had fallen over because it was raining in a city three hundred kilometers away.

He had gone to find the training staff.

I had checked the alert under the desk. Nagazora again. Smaller than the big one, but longer.

"What exactly happens if I die?"

[Before you complete an objective, the run resets. Your soul pays for the death.]

"Back to the beginning?"

[Back to the setup. You may change the choices that are available.]

"And once I complete one?"

[The next death ends the run successfully. You settle what you earned.]

"So I can't complete an easy objective, die, and use the next attempt for the others."

[You can try. You'll be doing it from your bed.]

I wrote that down on the back of an old maintenance schedule. There were four facts I wanted in front of me before I went in, and I numbered them with Mori's pen.

Death before success meant a reset. Death after success meant settlement. Memories came back when I woke. Damage did not.

"What about Torn?"

[You won't be starting another heroine run in that condition. An attempt already underway follows its own reset rules. At Hollow, you get the final attempt I told you about.]

"And I can end a failed run?"

[Yes.]

I tapped the pen against the paper.

"One failed death," I said. "Then I reconsider."

[Of course.]

I looked up at the ceiling.

"That sounded like you didn't believe me."

[You haven't done anything yet.]

I set my alarm for six and plugged the phone in. The charging cable was beginning to split near the connector. I turned it until the battery symbol appeared, put the maintenance schedule where I could see it when I woke, and got under the blanket.

Mei. I was going to meet Raiden Mei.

The thought arrived separately from everything I had been calculating, and I had to sit up again. I had spent five years on an island with Theresa Apocalypse walking around above my head and had managed to speak to her twice. I had kept a piece of candy in my drawer for most of those five years. Now I was about to go somewhere that had Mei in it, close enough to talk to, and I could apparently do something about what was going to happen to her.

I turned the lamp off before I could find a reason to open the shop again.

"Yes."

---

The dark place had acquired a chair.

It was an ordinary school chair, metal legs and a wooden seat with a split in the varnish. I looked behind it for a desk. There was nothing.

[For the planning.]

"Very considerate."

[I did notice you tend to sit down for these.]

I sat. A row of cards appeared at eye level.

```

```

I read Cold Tolerance with some feeling.

"You're enjoying that one."

[It has its uses.]

The first four runs had taught me things. One of them was that getting a good gift in a run was different from getting to keep it. Another was that an ability was only as useful as the person who chose the situation in which to use it.

This situation involved talking to a girl.

I picked Calm Voice and Honest Face.

[Locked for this heroine run.]

"That includes the resets?"

[The whole run. You don't get five new cards every time you fall down the stairs.]

"I hadn't asked."

[You were looking at the reroll price.]

The cards folded into a panel.

```
[Heroine simulation: Raiden Mei]
[Immediate immersion at an adult life node]

Identity: Eros
Age at immersion: 18
Heroine age at immersion: 18
Origin: Hangzhou
Admission: Chiba Academy scholarship
Immersion date: October 1, 2013

[Fixed conditions]
Identity and age
Scholarship admission
Transfer date and school year
Existing histories of other characters
```

Eighteen. I looked at my hands. They were still twenty-two in the dark place.

"Am I losing my gifts?"

[No. Your body at entry will fit the identity. Your permanent gifts and retained experience remain.]

"My fitness?"

[Within an ordinary eighteen-year-old body. You may choose some preparation.]

"And nobody's going to ask why I look exactly like an engineer working for Schicksal?"

[This life has its own records. You aren't sharing it with another version of yourself.]

The panel slid to the left, and a line of dates unrolled across the empty space.

[2008. You are offered a place in a school language program.]

Three choices appeared under it. Weekend classes. A language-event volunteer group. Private study.

I touched the volunteer group. A small picture formed, school tables under a cloth awning, a younger pair of hands passing out programs. A line appeared.

[You learn to approach strangers without waiting to be introduced. Your Japanese improves through practice.]

I could remember the people without having been one of them. The memory had the completeness of an address I had just read, and none of the feeling of living there. There was a woman who corrected how I introduced myself. There was a boy who always arrived late. Neither had a face I could hold for more than a moment.

The dates continued.

[2011. You can take one regular extracurricular responsibility.]

Language events again. Office assistance. Audiovisual work.

I picked the language events. It looked more relevant than learning which printer in a staff room needed to be kicked.

[2013, August. You receive confirmation of the transfer.]

There were forms. There was an allowance that was considerably smaller than I wanted. There was a choice between a boarding room with meals and a small rented room without them.

"Can I choose a richer family?"

[That node says fixed. You can ask it more charmingly if you like.]

I picked the boarding room. Food I did not have to make was food I could not forget to eat.

[Preparation before transfer.]

Social practice. School procedures. Conditioning.

I looked at Conditioning. My last normal run had ended with my hands going somewhere my feet could not follow.

Then I looked at the name at the top of the panel.

Social practice.

The line ended at October first, outside a classroom door.

[How will you introduce yourself?]

There were options. Quietly. Offer to help. Make yourself memorable.

"Well," I said.

[I thought you might.]

I selected the third.

Two objectives opened.

```
[Love]
Share a kiss that Raiden Mei freely returns as herself.

[Fate]
Help Raiden Mei regain sustained conscious control during the artificial eruption.
```

I read the first one several times.

"Both?"

[One is enough for success. Each additional objective improves the settlement.]

"And if I just go up to her and kiss her?"

[You can read the line again.]

"I meant if she likes it."

[Then perhaps you'll find out.]

There were three meters below Mei's name, all at their starting levels. Affection, Trust, Corruption. I knew what the first two were. I asked about the third.

[Changes in what she'll accept, depend on, or do. It is separate from whether she likes you.]

"So high isn't necessarily good."

[That rather depends on what you're trying to accomplish.]

I looked at the little window showing the classroom door. There was a strip of frosted glass in it and a paper sign above it. Someone on the other side laughed.

"Can I see what happens if I choose differently?"

[After it happens.]

"Fine."

[Begin immersion?]

I stood up. The chair disappeared before I could put it back under anything.

---

"Your name," said the man next to me.

"What?"

"For the board. How would you like it written?"

I was holding a thin folder. The edge had been bent where my thumb pressed it. There was a school tie at my throat and a new shirt scratching the back of my neck. From the classroom came the noise of people who had been left alone for slightly too long.

Mr. Fujita had a piece of chalk in his hand. I knew his name because he had introduced himself a few minutes earlier, before I had properly been there to hear it.

"Eros," I said.

"Yes. The spelling."

I spelled it. He wrote it on a scrap of paper, squinted at it and showed it to me.

"Like that?"

"Yes, sir."

"You can put the folder on my desk once we're in. There isn't much to say. A few words about yourself. Try to speak up. The ones at the back will complain even if they hear you."

He opened the door.

I followed him into a room with windows along one side and a row of lockers at the back. Someone stopped leaning against a desk and hurried into a chair. Someone else was still finishing a sentence about a television program. The room smelled of floor wax, paper and the sharp sweet smell of somebody's hair product.

Fujita put his attendance book down.

"All right. We have a transfer student."

I had imagined the moment. Of course I had. In the dark place, while reading the objectives, I had managed to imagine several versions of it, most of them dignified. I was going to be pleasant and mildly interesting, perhaps make a little joke about getting lost, then find somewhere to sit and spend a sensible amount of time getting to know her.

She sat three rows back, toward the windows.

Her hair was longer than I had thought it would be. It was tied up behind her, dark until the daylight caught it and turned the edges faintly violet. One hand rested on an open notebook. She had been writing when we came in. Now she had put her pen down and was looking at me with the same polite interest as everyone else.

I knew her face and did not know it. The proportions were different in small ways that my brain kept trying to correct. She had a tiny crease between her brows from the light, and a mark from her pen on the side of one finger.

Raiden Mei was in a classroom looking at me, and I had forgotten the prepared sentence.

"This is Eros," Fujita said, writing my name on the board. "He's joining us from Hangzhou on a scholarship. I expect you'll help him settle in."

Mei smiled.

It was a small, ordinary welcome to a stranger, and I had spent money on pictures of that smile.

"Go on," Fujita said.

"My name is Eros."

A boy near the front raised his hand.

"That part's on the board."

A few people laughed.

"Yes," I said. "It is."

Fujita turned his head slightly toward me. There was still time to say something about my interests. Reading. Computers. A sensible sport. Any of those would have been fine.

"Raiden Mei," I said. "I like you. Would you go out with me?"

The boy at the front put his hand over his mouth.

For a few seconds nothing happened. The chalk in Fujita's hand made a small clicking noise against his wedding ring. Mei's expression had stopped exactly where the smile had been.

Then a girl behind her leaned forward and whispered something, and half the room seemed to find permission to breathe.

"Have you met?" Fujita asked.

"No," Mei said.

She answered him first. That was worse, somehow, than anything she could have said to me.

Then she looked at me.

"Thank you, but no."

Her voice was very composed. I kept mine quiet and tried to hold on to the rest of the sentence.

"All right."

Fujita put the chalk down.

"Do you have anything to tell us about your studies?"

I considered this.

"I'm good with computers."

"Good. We'll try to make use of that rather than the other thing. There's a desk in the second row."

He pointed. It was on the opposite side of the room from Mei.

I walked to it while someone whispered "scholarship" in a tone that suggested the standards had slipped. The boy who had spoken earlier turned in his chair as I sat.

"Toru Matsuda," he said. "That was really something."

"Eros."

"I know."

I took out my notebook and put it on the desk. My hand was perfectly steady. That was one of the nice things about panic. If it went on for long enough, eventually you ran out of immediate things to do with it.

*What was that?* I asked.

[You selected memorable.]

*I was going to introduce myself normally.*

[You began very well.]

Fujita opened the attendance book. Around the room people returned to their notes and their private conversations. Mei had picked up her pen.

I looked at the empty margin of my notebook.

She had talked to me. That was an astonishingly small thing to be pleased about, considering what had just happened. I was pleased anyway.

---

At lunch, Toru brought three people over to my desk.

"Do it again," he said.

"What?"

"The introduction."

"No."

"That calm face, too. How did you do that?"

"It's just my face."

One of the boys said something about the Lightning Queen, and the others laughed. I looked across the room. Mei was talking to the girl who had leaned toward her earlier, a neat girl with a little silver decoration on her phone. The girl had both hands around Mei's wrist and seemed to be asking for something.

"Lightning Queen?" I asked.

Toru pulled a chair over and sat on it backward.

"You picked well."

"I know."

"No, I mean for your first morning. Was it a dare?"

"No."

"You really just walked in and did that?"

"Yes."

He looked delighted.

"She's Raiden Mei."

"I'd gathered that."

"ME Corp. Her dad."

"I know who her father is."

"Then you know the part where she doesn't have to say yes to random people who show up in class."

I looked at him.

"Does anyone?"

One of the other boys laughed. Toru pointed at him without looking away from me.

"That's not what I meant. Listen. There was a whole week last year where people kept leaving things on her desk. Flowers and letters and stuff. She gave everything back. Every single one. Even the food."

"Perhaps the food wasn't good."

"One guy played a song outside her classroom."

"What happened?"

"She shut the window."

I liked her more.

Toru watched my face and shook his head.

"Oh, you're in trouble."

By then the room had started to empty. Mei was collecting papers into a folder while the silver-phone girl talked. I took my lunch from the bag the boarding-house woman had given me. She had written my room number on it in large black figures.

"You're not going to ask her again, are you?" Toru said.

"Not during lunch."

He slapped the back of his chair. The others laughed, and he told someone outside the door that the transfer student was serious.

I opened the lunch and discovered that I had chosen correctly about the boarding room. There was rice, chicken, and something green that had been cooked by somebody who knew what they were doing. I ate it while finding the next class in the timetable.

At the end of lunch Mei came over.

She waited for Toru to move the chair out of her path. He did it slowly, looking from her to me.

"Could I speak to you?"

"Yes."

Her fingers tightened on the folder.

"I don't know how people usually introduce themselves at your old school," she said. "But that was difficult."

"I'm sorry."

"All right."

She waited. I had no idea what she was waiting for.

"Was there something else?"

"No. I just wanted you to know."

"Okay."

She looked at me for another moment, then nodded and went back to her desk.

Toru leaned in.

"What did she say?"

"That I made things difficult."

"She's nice. She could have been worse."

I watched her sit down and take out her books. She had given me a chance to understand something without humiliating me in front of the room.

I had done a very stupid thing that morning. It had taken perhaps five seconds. It had also got me two conversations with Raiden Mei, which was two more than I had ever had before.

I finished my lunch, folded the paper bag, and put it away. There was a timetable to learn and a school map in the folder, and for the rest of that first afternoon I had quite enough to do without improving on my introduction.

---

The next morning I arrived early enough to be respectable.

This took some doing. My boarding room was on the third floor of a house with a staircase that had been built for people smaller than the landlady. She came up it at six-thirty every morning to make sure we had remembered breakfast, and I had discovered that if I was already in the bathroom she would ask about my bowels through the door. It was easier to get up before her.

I walked to school with the map folded in my pocket. There was a bakery near the station and a narrow shop that sold batteries, umbrellas, and apparently every kind of adhesive known to Japan. I went into the second one and bought a pen. I had brought three notebooks and no pen that worked.

Mei came through the gate just as I was putting the change away.

A black car stopped at the curb. A man with gray hair got out and opened the rear door, and she spoke to him before stepping onto the pavement. He handed her a cloth bag. She bowed her head slightly in thanks.

I knew about the chauffeur. I had read about him somewhere. Seeing him check that she had her bag made me feel oddly intrusive.

She saw me waiting by the gate.

"Good morning," she said.

"Good morning. Would you go out with me?"

"No."

"Okay."

She adjusted the strap of her bag.

"Was that why you were standing here?"

"I was buying a pen."

I held it up.

"Then why ask?"

"Because you arrived."

She looked at the pen, then at the shop, then back at me.

"I have to go to the staff room."

"Which one? There seem to be several."

"The one where the teachers are."

"Right."

She walked ahead. I waited until she was a few steps away before following, because I had enough sense to understand that walking beside her now would make my morning worse.

At the entrance I changed my shoes and found that someone had put a little drawing of a crown on the outside of my locker. Toru was leaning against the next row.

"Did you?"

"Did I what?"

"Ask again."

"Yes."

"What did she say?"

"No."

He counted on his fingers, very slowly.

"Two."

"You'll run out of those."

"Then I'll ask for help."

I peeled the crown off. It had been drawn with some care. Under it, in small letters, somebody had written good luck in English.

---

By Friday I knew where most of the rooms were and had been rejected four times.

The fourth one was my fault in a different fashion. I had asked while Mei was holding a large stack of exercise books. She had said no and then asked whether I intended to watch her carry them.

I took the top half of the stack.

She led me through a corridor into a staff room with an electric heater running underneath an open window. A teacher glanced up, recognized her, and smiled.

"Raiden. Thank you. On the table."

Mei put her books down. The teacher asked whether her father had enjoyed a recent event. She gave an answer that sounded friendly and had almost no information in it.

On the way out, two girls stopped her to ask about a school fundraiser.

"Have you called them?"

"I sent the request yesterday," Mei said.

"And?"

"They haven't answered yet."

"But they will, right?"

"I don't know."

The taller girl laughed and squeezed her arm.

"They always answer you."

They went on. Mei watched them for a moment, then continued toward the stairs.

"Does that happen a lot?" I asked.

"What?"

"People asking you to make somebody else answer them."

She glanced at me.

"Sometimes."

"Seems tiring."

"It isn't usually difficult."

That was not what I had asked. She knew it too, because she changed the subject.

"Have you found a club?"

"Not yet."

"You should look at the list."

"Which one's yours?"

"Kendo."

"I know."

Her head turned.

"I saw the tournament results," I said. It was a perfectly respectable answer because I had.

"Oh."

"First place."

"Yes."

"Was it close?"

"No."

There was no vanity in it. I laughed, and after a second she did too.

The club room was in a building behind the gym. I found it that afternoon, partly because she had suggested looking at clubs and partly because I wanted to see her with a sword.

I was not the only person who had had that idea. Two boys stood near the entrance with the vague expressions of people who had come to watch a friend and could not name the friend. A girl collecting attendance asked whether I wanted to join.

"I'd like to watch first."

"Sit there. Don't block the doorway."

I sat on a bench and took my shoes off.

Training was mostly much less exciting than the videos. There were drills and corrections and a great deal of moving forward and backward. Mei wore protective gear and looked like everyone else until she moved. Then somebody's hands were in the wrong place and she had already done something about it.

I tried to follow her feet. Years in the forest had taught me to notice a bad stance and a slow turn. Mine had got me killed recently. Watching somebody who had been taught properly was useful, even when I was also being a fool.

Afterward she came over carrying her helmet.

"Are you joining?"

"I haven't decided."

"If you join, you have to practice."

"I assumed there'd be a viewing membership."

"No."

"You're very consistent."

Her hair had come loose at one side and stuck to her cheek. She pushed it away with the back of her wrist.

"Would you like the form?"

I looked at the class schedule pinned to the wall. Training, three afternoons a week. Extra practice before competitions. I thought about running in the mornings, about the eruption and what three months of getting hit with bamboo might buy me.

Then I thought about how little of a sword fight I could survive anyway.

"I'll think about it."

"All right."

She turned to go.

"Mei."

She looked back, eyebrows rising at the name.

"Would you like to get a drink?"

"No, thank you."

"I mean from the machine. You look tired."

"Then you can ask that."

"Would you like a drink from the machine because you've been practicing for two hours?"

She considered me.

"Water."

I bought two bottles. She took hers, thanked me, and stayed beside the machine while she opened it.

It was an excellent afternoon. I learned almost nothing about swordsmanship and paid a hundred and twenty yen to stand beside a girl drinking water, which would have been embarrassing to explain to anyone in my first life.

---

The fundraiser was what got me her number.

There were collection tins in the staff room and a donation page on the school website that did not work properly. I found the problem because a teacher asked whether I was the new student who knew about computers, and I had not yet learned to say that I knew a different kind of computers.

The form accepted donations, then sent the confirmation to the address from the previous entry.

I stopped it, told the teacher, and spent my afternoon sorting out which acknowledgments had gone to which parents. Mei came in at four carrying a folder.

"Is it bad?"

"Nobody's lost money. Some people have been thanked for other people's money."

"Oh."

"Your school has a very optimistic approach to testing."

She put the folder down and sat at the next computer.

"Can I help?"

"Yes. Please."

She read names out while I matched them against transactions. She knew most of the families. A name that meant nothing to me would make her stop and say the first initial was wrong, or that those two people were married and should be on the same letter.

After half an hour she asked whether I had eaten.

"At lunch."

"It's nearly five."

"That's still the same day."

She went out and came back with a small packet of crackers.

"I have to leave at five-thirty," she said.

"I didn't ask you to stay."

"I know."

I took a cracker and got crumbs in the keyboard. She handed me a tissue before saying anything.

The donation page was fixed by the time the sky outside the staff room windows had gone dark. We had a list of apologies to send and a considerably shorter list of people who might need to be called. Mei checked the second list twice.

"You can send it to me," she said.

"How?"

She gave me her number. I typed it into the work sheet before remembering that this was a school computer.

"Your phone," she said.

"Yes. Of course."

I took it out too fast and dropped it under the desk.

She covered her mouth. Her shoulders moved.

"I'm usually better than this."

"I believe you."

That was Honest Face doing its job, perhaps. Or she was simply kind. I got the phone out, cleaned dust off the back, and put the number in.

When I sent the file, her own phone lit up on the desk.

"There," she said. "Now I can finish it at home."

"Would you like to go out with me?"

"No."

"Right. Thought I'd check."

She shook her head and started packing the folder. I stayed to wipe the crackers out of the keyboard because she had already seen enough.

At the school gate the chauffeur was waiting. Mei introduced him as Mr. Tanabe and said I had been helping with the donation page. He thanked me with the polite seriousness of an adult who had heard many more important introductions that day.

As he opened the car door, she looked back.

"Please don't send that question with the file."

"Separate message?"

"Eros."

"Okay."

She got in. Mr. Tanabe shut the door, and I walked back to the boarding house with a ridiculous desire to tell somebody what had happened.

The System kept quiet until I had taken my shoes off.

[You could ask whether she has a favorite color.]

*I know her favorite color.*

[Do you?]

I stopped with one shoe in my hand. I could remember outfits. I could remember equipment and art and dialogue about things she liked to cook. I could not remember the answer to that question.

*Purple, probably.*

[An ambitious deduction.]

I put the shoe down and went upstairs. The landlady asked whether I wanted extra rice, and for once I remembered to answer before she had to ask again.

---

By the third week, Toru had acquired a notebook.

He kept the tally in the back because Fujita had confiscated the sheet of paper he had first pinned to my desk. Beside each number he had written a brief description. Gate. Books. Vending machine. Perfectly normal Thursday.

"I don't see why the place matters," I said.

"Details make it scientific."

"That's not what makes things scientific."

He clicked his pen.

"What would you call it?"

"A hobby you should keep to yourself."

He had asked for the number because he wanted to know whether I was serious. I told him I was. He pointed out that I had been told no on every occasion.

"I noticed."

"So why?"

"Because I like her."

"Lots of people like her."

"Then she has plenty of practice answering."

He laughed and leaned back in his chair until the front legs left the floor.

"You'll stop."

"When?"

"When you get bored."

"I've been doing software maintenance for ten years."

"What?"

"Never mind."

He let the chair fall forward.

"By New Year," he said.

"What?"

"You'll stop by New Year."

I looked at the notebook. He was enjoying this very much. Behind him the girl with the silver phone, Sayaka, was showing another girl something on her screen. That was Natsumi. They were always together until Mei arrived, then they became three people who had been waiting for one another all morning.

"Five hundred yen," Toru said. "You won't get a date by New Year."

"Is this how you spend your scholarship?"

"I don't have a scholarship."

"Then stop trying to spend mine."

"Five hundred. A proper date. Her saying she'll help you with something doesn't count."

I knew I should tell him to put the notebook away. It was an irritating joke, and he was the sort of boy who would repeat it to anybody willing to listen.

I also knew that I could afford five hundred yen, and that being laughed at was considerably easier when I could give the impression I had chosen to be funny.

"Fine."

He held out his hand.

I shook it.

Natsumi had her phone raised. When she lowered it, she smiled at Toru and asked whether we had agreed on a date for the next failure. He asked her to send him the clip.

---

In November, Mei stopped giving the same answer.

"No, I'm busy."

"No. We have a test."

"No, and you should be studying for the test."

"No, you cannot count buying stationery together as a date."

That last one had been worth trying. We both needed notebooks. It was a sensible use of an afternoon.

She did sometimes talk afterward. I learned that the list of books she wanted to read was longer than the time she had available, and that she disliked being asked whether she was allowed to do ordinary things. She ate quickly when she was alone and slowly when there were people watching.
