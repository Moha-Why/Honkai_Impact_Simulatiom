# Chapter 3: The Lightning Queen

On the fourth of February the Nagazora line was on every screen in the building.

Somebody in the watch room had put it up on the big wall display at the end of the corridor, where it usually showed the weather over the Pacific, and nobody had taken it down. I walked past it four times on the way to the coffee machine. The spike from the night before sat at the top of it like a tooth, six point four, and since then the line hadn't come down as it had after every other spike in December and January. It hovered around five and twitched.

Yoon was in the watch room when I went down at ten with a calibration I didn't need to do. She had her feet up on the desk and her headset around her neck, and she was eating dried squid out of a packet without looking at it.

"You again," she said.

"Me again."

"Your firmware is fine. It's been fine for two years." She pointed a strip of squid at the screen. "That isn't."

"Has she called?"

Yoon knew who I meant. Everybody on the island said "she" now and meant the principal. "Twice. She asked if the two birds outside the city were healthy. They're healthy. They're the healthiest birds in Japan." She chewed. "Then she asked me if I'd ever seen a line do that, and I said no, and she said thank you, and hung up."

I watched the line for a while. Five point one. Five point three. Ten was where the people inside it would stop being people.

"Go away, Eros," Yoon said, not unkindly. "You make it worse when you stare at it."

On the way back up I stopped at the window on the landing. From there you could see the north tower, and the Hyperion moored alongside it with its lights on in the middle of the morning, the engines making a low sound you felt in the railing more than heard. A tender was going out to it across the water with a pallet of something under a tarpaulin. Twelve hours' notice had become two at the end of January, and the engines hadn't stopped since.

Asami found me at lunch, eating instant noodles at my desk.

"You look like a man who's been told his pet died," she said, and sat on the edge of the desk and took one of my chopsticks to stir my noodles, which she did when she thought I wasn't eating them. "Is it the Nagazora thing?"

"It's a lot of things."

"It's the Nagazora thing." She gave me the chopstick back. "They've put the Valkyries on the north tower on rotation. Four hours on, four off. Nobody's said what for." She looked at me for a moment longer than usual. "You don't know anything, do you? Tell me you don't know anything."

"I write firmware, Asami."

"That's what I keep telling people." She stood up and patted my head, which she also did when she thought I wasn't eating. "Eat. Then go home and sleep. You've got eyes like a raccoon."

I ate. I went back to work. I fixed a bug I'd been ignoring since Christmas, and I answered an email from Mori about the spring budget, and the whole time I was doing the math, the same way I'd done it every hour since one in the morning on the third.

It was simple math, and that was what bothered me about it. I knew Mei's whole story, from the arrest to the roof. I'd played it so many times in my first life that I could have drawn the classroom from memory, the third desk by the window, the cherry tree outside that didn't flower in the game because the game only ever showed it in winter. It was the best-written route HI3 had ever had. Everyone said so. A girl who had everything, who lost all of it in one day in November, and the long cold months after that when the people who had loved her name stopped loving her, until a loud white-haired girl who'd never had anything sat down at her desk and ate her lunch.

The System had told me on the first night that the simulations weren't real. It had said it in those exact words, twice, because I'd made it say them again, word for word, as you'd make a vendor put a promise in writing. Nothing that happened in there could touch anyone out here. It was a branch of nothing. A save file.

So nothing I did in there could hurt anyone.

I turned that over all afternoon, and every time I came back to it, it was still true, and every time it was still true, I felt something open up underneath it, like a door in a floor.

I left at six. I ran the seawall a second time, which I never did, and showered, and sat on the end of the bed with my hair wet and the phone in my hand. Five point eight. Five point nine. Outside the glass Shiomi's lights were on along the far shore, and the Hyperion was a long dark shape at the edge of the island with its running lights blinking red.

The System hadn't said a word since "Okay." It had been nearly two days. I'd never had the last word with it for that long, and I'd stopped enjoying it somewhere around noon.

"All right," I said to the room. "Yes."

Nothing happened for a second. Then the dark went white, and there wasn't a word of greeting, only the screen.

An hour ago I'd died under those lights, on the floor of a dojo, with my head in somebody's lap.

[Good morning,] said the System.

"Was any of it real?" I said.

[Does it matter?] it said lightly. [You'd do it again.]

The ship went north over the sea, getting smaller. Its lights went out over the horizon one row at a time, the windows first and then the running lights, until there was only the searchlight, and then not that. I stood at the glass with my hands on the railing and watched the place where it had been until the sky over Shiomi started to go gray.
