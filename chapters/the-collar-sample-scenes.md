## 2. The wardrobe

---

Before I went back over the wall that morning, I asked her what else she had to wear.

"This," she said. She was still kneeling, with the tag in the hollow of her throat. "And my coat. They took the rest on the third day, in boxes. A tailor made them, and the company paid him." She was looking at the boards in front of my feet. "One hundred and fifty to one hundred and seventy-three."

"Then I'll bring you some."

She lifted her eyes at that, as far as my chin, and didn't ask what.

The boy had nineteen thousand yen in an envelope inside his dictionary, from five years of Saturdays at the noodle stall and whatever the scholarship paid for books that he hadn't spent on books. I took all of it down the hill that afternoon.

The pet shop first. The girl at the counter remembered me and asked if he'd liked the tag. I said he had. On the back wall, past the fish, there was a shelf for big dogs, and on it a step-in harness of red nylon webbing for a breed the size of a man, with black plastic buckles and a steel ring at the back, and a leash to match. I took two of the harnesses, for the straps. From the shelf for small dogs I took two red collars that would go round a wrist, and a fine steel choke chain. I held one of the harnesses up against myself in the aisle to judge the straps, and a woman with a poodle looked at me and then didn't.

[Is it for the same dog?] said the System.

Under the arches by the station there was a shop that sold other people's clothes by the kilo. In the back, on a rail of vinyl that smelled of somebody's cupboard, I found a white bodysuit, long-sleeved and high-necked, with a zip down the back and a stain on one cuff, for twelve hundred yen. It covered everything. That was why I bought it. Two doors down, the hundred-yen shop had a clear plastic raincoat in a packet and a roll of purple electrical tape.
