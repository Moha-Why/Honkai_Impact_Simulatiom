## 1. The labels and the collar

---

The receiver's men were in the house for three days.

There were four of them, in gray suits, with clipboards and a roll of wire and a cardboard box of white tags, and they took their shoes off at the Raidens' front door every morning and worked all day in their socks. I watched them from my window above the wall when I came back from school. They put a tag on each of the stone lanterns in the garden. They put one on the stone basin by the pond, and one, after a long discussion on the lawn, on the plum tree.

"They put a number on a tree," Mrs. Okada said on the second evening, when I came down to pay the rent. She was at her kitchen window with her tea going cold in her hands. "Her mother planted that tree."

"Who gets it?"

"Whoever buys the house, student. It goes with the house." She put the envelope in her apron without counting it. "The girl walks round after them with her hands behind her back. She thanks them at the door."

---

On the third night every light in the Raiden house came on at two in the morning.

I'd watched it happen in other files, from the bottom of her street and from this same window. It was close enough to read by. The whole long front of the house went white at once, every window, and the stone lanterns in the garden lit, which had no bulbs in them, and the frost on the lawn shone blue under them. It held for about five seconds. Then all of it went out together, and the garden was black except for the dojo at the far end, where somebody had lit the lamp.

I went over the wall.

[Oh,] said the System. [At last.]

The frost broke under my shoes. A tag hung from the nearest lantern on a twist of wire, a white card like the ones they tie to suitcases, with a number on it in red and a stamp across the number. Thirty-one. The next lantern along was thirty-two. The plum tree by the dojo had one on its lowest branch, turning slowly on its wire in the cold. Forty-four.

The doors of the dojo were open. She was kneeling in the middle of the floor in her coat, in seiza, facing the post, with the lamp on the boards beside her. There was a tag on the lamp, and one on the sword rack, and one on the long silk bag on the top shelf of the rack where her father's sword lived. She didn't turn round when I came up onto the step.

"You're the student," she said. "From Mrs. Okada's."

"Yes."

"I told you no."

"You did."

She didn't send me away. She didn't say anything else for a while either, and I stood on the step in the cold and waited, because waiting was what I'd come over the wall to do.

"They're very polite," she said at last. "They bow when they come in. They took their shoes off at the door every morning, all four of them, and then they walked round my house in their socks and put a number on everything in it." Her voice was quite level. It was the voice she kept for teachers. "The piano is forty. My mother's chair is twelve. I asked the young one what the tree was worth, and he looked it up in his book and told me, and then he apologized."

She lifted one hand to her throat.

Her coat was open over the mended harness, the ribbon she'd tied over her breasts where the lacquer had cracked away and the three safety pins holding the split seam together over her bare ribs. At the base of her throat, where the violet stone had sat in her collar every day since September, there was a small empty ring of metal.

"They took that on the second day," she said. "The stone was bought with the company's money. They had a little tool for it." Her fingers stayed on the ring. "It's two hundred and eleven."

I took her in as the men in socks would have, item by item, for the book. It was the only way of looking at anything I had left. The ribbon, and the bare skin between the lapels, white in the lamplight, with the pulse going under the empty ring. My body took its own inventory, at its own speed, and the rest of me wrote it down.

"Everything in this house has a number on it," she said. "Everything I touch. The cup I drink from in the morning is ninety-six." She turned her head and looked at me over her shoulder for the first time, and her eyes were dry and very wide. "Except me."

I took my shoes off and came in and knelt on the boards behind her, an arm's length back, where I'd knelt every morning for four months in the last file. I had that as a fact. I couldn't remember the boards being this cold.

"On Saturday you said you wanted me," she said, to the post.

"Yes."

"You said it as if I were a thing. Not to go out with. Not to sit with at lunch." She was very still. "Me."

"Yes."

"Everyone who comes close to me now wants something."

They were her father's words. The System had given them to me in the white after the fourth file, word for word, from a visiting room I'd never been in, and in all the files since I'd never once heard her say them herself.

"Mr. Tanabe rings every evening at seven and asks if I've eaten," she said. "I can't see what he wants. I lie awake trying to see it." Her hand closed on the empty ring. "The men in socks want two hundred and eleven things, and they told me every one. You're the only other person who said what it was."

"I'll say it again, if you like."

"Yes." Her voice dropped, and the courtesy went out of it. "All of it. Properly. I don't want to find anything out afterward."

So I told her, in the voice I'd have used to read out a spec.

"You'd be mine," I said. "I don't mean a girlfriend. You'd do what I tell you. You'd eat when I say and sleep when I say, and you wouldn't decide anything I hadn't let you decide, including who you talk to. Nobody else would get to touch you, or put a number on you."

She didn't move.

"And you wouldn't have to hold anything up any more," I said. "Not this house, and not yourself." I looked at the lamp with its tag. "Every light in the house came on at two o'clock tonight. I live above the wall."

Her back went rigid. Her hand came down off the ring and took hold of her other wrist, hard, in her lap.

"If you're mine," I said, "whatever does that doesn't get to decide anything either. It'll have to ask me first."

"And if it doesn't ask?"

"Then you'll do as you're told. And so will it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the hill a dog barked twice and stopped. She sat with her wrist in her hand and looked at the post with its number on it, and I knelt behind her and let her take as long as she needed. She did her own sums. I'd learned that in other files, at some cost, though the cost had gone out of me with most of the rest.

"Not at school," she said at last.

"Outside the gate, then."

"And my father is never to know."

"He won't hear it from me."

She let go of her wrist. She turned round on her knees on the boards, all the way round, slowly, until she was facing me in the lamplight with her hands on her thighs and her back straight. Then she lifted her chin, so the empty ring at her throat caught the light.

"Then put your name on it," she said.

---

The pet shop by the station did engraving while you waited. I'd walked past the machine in its window every day since September.

The girl at the counter had a cat asleep beside the till. She pushed a pad and a pencil across to me and asked what the dog was called.

"It's the owner's name," I said, and wrote it on the pad in capitals.

It took four minutes. It was a plain brass disc the size of a coin, with a hole at the top and a split ring through the hole, and it cost six hundred and fifty yen. She put it in a paper bag with a bone printed on it and said she hoped he'd like it.

[Six hundred and fifty yen,] said the System, as I went out into the cold. [I've seen you spend more on noodles.]

---

On Saturday the post was silent at five.

I went round by the street and through the gap in the hedge. The lamp was lit and the doors were open, and she was already there, kneeling in the middle of the boards in seiza in the mended harness, with her coat folded on the floor beside her as neatly as she'd have folded it for a grading. The cold had raised gooseflesh all down her bare arms, and she wasn't shivering, because she'd decided not to.

I came in and stood in front of her.

"Lift your chin," I said.

She lifted it.

The split ring was stiff. I had to open it with my thumbnail and walk it round through the empty ring at her throat, one coil and then the other. My hands had forty years of a knife in them from a forest in Hokkaido, and they were clumsy at it, and she held her chin up the whole time and didn't breathe. Then it was through. I let go. The tag swung once on its ring and settled in the hollow of her throat, where the stone had been.

[♪]

She couldn't see it. She lifted her hand and found it by touch, and turned it over between her finger and thumb, and read it with her fingertips, four letters, one at a time.

"What do I call you?" she said.

"You don't call me anything," I said. "When you've done what you're told, I'll say your name."

"And when I haven't?"

"Then I won't."

She thought about that, still holding the tag.

"Nobody says it any more," she said. "Not since November. Mr. Tanabe says young lady."

She put her hand back on her thigh and waited, with her back straight and her chin still up, for me to decide whether she'd done as she was told.

"Mei," I said.

Her eyes closed. A breath went out of her, long and slow, and behind me the frost on the step cracked in the cold. When she opened her eyes she didn't look at my face. She looked at the boards a little in front of my feet.

## 2. The wardrobe

---

Before I went back over the wall that morning, I asked her what else she had to wear.

"This," she said. She was still kneeling, with the tag in the hollow of her throat. "And my coat. They took the rest on the third day, in boxes. A tailor made them, and the company paid him." She was looking at the boards in front of my feet. "One hundred and fifty to one hundred and seventy-three."

"Then I'll bring you some."

She lifted her eyes at that, as far as my chin, and didn't ask what.

---

The boy had nineteen thousand yen in an envelope inside his dictionary, from five years of Saturdays at the noodle stall and whatever the scholarship paid for books that he hadn't spent on books. I took all of it down the hill that afternoon.

The pet shop first. The girl at the counter remembered me and asked if he'd liked the tag. I said he had. On the back wall, past the fish, there was a shelf for big dogs, and on it a step-in harness of red nylon webbing for a breed the size of a man, with black plastic buckles and a steel ring at the back and two padded panels for the chest, and a leash to match. It adjusted everywhere. I held it up against myself in the aisle to judge the straps, and a woman with a poodle looked at me and then didn't.

[Is it for the same dog?] said the System.

Under the arches by the station there was a shop that sold other people's clothes by the kilo. In the back, on a rail of vinyl that smelled of somebody's cupboard, I found a white bodysuit, long-sleeved and high-necked, with a zip down the back and a stain on one cuff, for twelve hundred yen. It covered everything. That was why I bought it. Two doors down, the hundred-yen shop had a clear plastic raincoat in a packet, and a roll of purple electrical tape.

[Is this from the folder?] said the System, while I paid for the tape.

I didn't have a single picture from the folder left. I had the filenames, four thousand of them, sorted by artist. My body had the rest.

"Near enough," I said.

The apron I already had. It was in the bottom of the boy's bag, where he'd kept it since his last Saturday at the stall, white cotton gone gray at the strings, with the stall's name printed across the chest in red.

---

Her bedroom was at the end of the upstairs corridor, and it had numbers on everything, like the rest of the house. The bed was eighty-eight. The mirror on its stand was sixty-one. The wardrobe doors stood open on a rail of empty hangers, and every hanger still had its white tag on a twist of wire, though what had hung on it was gone.

She was sitting on the edge of the bed in the mended harness with her hands in her lap. She'd swept the floor for me. I could see the lines the broom had left.

I put the bags on the bed and emptied them one at a time.

She looked at the paper bag with the bone printed on it for a while before she touched what had come out of it. Then she picked up the harness and turned it over in her hands, slowly, buckle by buckle, as she'd turn over a sword she hadn't made up her mind about.

"This is for a dog," she said.

"It's for you. Outside the gate. When we walk."

She laid it down on the bed beside her, straight, with the buckles lined up.

I shook out the white bodysuit and spread it face up on the bed.

"And this is for school."

Her head came up. "Not at school. You agreed."

"You said not at school," I said. "You won't kneel there, or ask me anything there. These are clothes. Every woman in that building is wearing something somebody chose."

She looked at me for a long time. She was going back over it word by word, what she'd said on her knees in the dojo at two in the morning, looking for the gap she'd meant to leave and hadn't.

"I should have said it properly," she said.

"Yes."

I took out my knife.

She watched it come out. She didn't move back. Her eyes followed the blade down to the vinyl and stopped there.

"Where?" she said.

I showed her with the point, without cutting yet, as she'd have shown a first-year where the strike went. Down the front from the collarbones to a hand below the navel, a hand's width open. The sides away from the armpits to the hips. An oval over each hip. The back, down to the base of the spine. She listened, and at each one she nodded once.

Then I cut it.

The boy's hands had never held a knife for anything but bread. Mine had forty years of skinning in them from a forest in Hokkaido, and they went through cheap vinyl as easily as through the belly of a hare, one long pull and the edges parting behind the point. It took four minutes. When I'd finished I ran the purple tape down every edge I'd made and pressed it flat with my thumb, and there was a design lying on her bed that I'd last seen as a filename.

"It will tear at the corners," she said. "Where you turned the blade." She was looking at the oval over the hip. "May I hem them?"

"Yes."

She got up and took a flat tin out of the drawer of her desk, which was seventy, and sat down again with a needle and black thread, and put a small hard stitch into the corner of every cut, one after another, with her head bent and her hair falling forward over one shoulder. She'd been mending everything she owned since November. She was good at it. When she'd finished she bit the thread off and put the needle back in the tin and shut the lid.

"Put it on," I said.

She stood up and undid the first ribbon. I didn't turn round, and she didn't ask me to.

---

When she was in it she stood in front of the mirror with sixty-one on its frame and looked.

The cut down the front showed the inner curves of her tits and her bare stomach to a hand below her navel, with the purple tape for a frame. The sides were open from her armpits to her hips, so that every time she moved her arm the outer curve of a breast showed and went. The ovals over her hips were bare skin in white. When she turned to see her back in the glass it was bare to the base of her spine, and below that the vinyl was pulled tight over her ass, as the folder had always drawn it and the official art never had. At her throat, on the ring where the stone had been, there was a brass disc with my name on it.

I was hard, and had been since the first ribbon. I wanted to fuck her. It was a fact, like the lot numbers, and I put it with the others and went on looking.

She looked at herself for a long time, very straight. Then she looked at me in the glass.

"Is this what you want to look at?"

"Yes."

"Then I will wear it." She turned from side to side, once each way, and watched the sides open and close in the mirror. "Sayaka will laugh. All of them will. They'll think I can't afford a tailor."

"You can't."

"No." Something moved at the corner of her mouth that I hadn't seen there since September. "They'll laugh, and I'll know who chose it, and they won't."

She put her hand up to the tag and turned it over once and let it fall.

The apron and the raincoat were still on the bed. She picked up the apron and read the stall's name on the chest.

"When you cook," I said.

"For whom?"

"Me."

"Only you," she said, and folded it in four and laid it on the pillow. Then she held the raincoat up to the window, where the garden showed straight through it. "And this?"

"For the gate. So I can see you from my window."

She folded that too. Then she picked up the red leash and turned the clip over in her fingers, and held it out to me across the bed, clip first.

"Outside the gate," she said.

I took it.
