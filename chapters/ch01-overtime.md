# Chapter 1: Overtime

The release was due at nine in the morning. At three, the office belonged to me and the vending machine.

I had four cans of the green energy drink lined up next to my keyboard. Three were empty and the fourth was going warm. The build had failed eleven times since midnight, and on my main monitor the twelfth attempt was crawling through its tests.

On my second monitor, Honkai Impact 3rd was open on the event screen. Every time I started a build, I played a stage while it ran, because the event shop closed in a few hours and I was still short on currency for the last item. A build took about eight minutes and a stage took about four. I'd worked out the timing hours ago, and I'd stopped enjoying it at around the same time.

On the laptop off to the side I had a forum thread open. A stranger with a cat-girl avatar was explaining to me that I didn't understand how Herrscher authorities worked.

I understood exactly how they worked. I had written four paragraphs on it, with sources, including two screenshots from the story and a photo of a page from an artbook I'd paid import shipping for. He had answered with one line and a laughing emoji, and eleven people had liked his reply.

I typed a fifth paragraph, read it back, and deleted it. Then I typed it again.

The build failed. A null pointer in a module I hadn't touched in two years, written by a man named Dennis who had quit to open a coffee shop. I'd been to the coffee shop once. The coffee was fine. I opened Dennis's code and started reading it with the kind of hate that keeps you awake better than caffeine does.

Around half past three my chest started to feel tight.

It was a dull pressure right in the middle, as if someone had put the flat of a hand on my breastbone and was leaning on it. I'd had something like it before, during the last two crunches, and it went away if I stood up and walked around. So I stood up and walked to the window. The street below was empty except for a taxi with its light on and nobody to pick up. I rolled my shoulders. I breathed in through my nose and out through my mouth, which a video had told me to do once.

The pressure stayed. My left arm started to feel heavy, and then from the elbow down it went numb, with pins and needles in the fingers. I shook it out. I told myself it was the energy drinks. The can said not to drink more than two a day, and I'd had three and a half, and a company that sold energy drinks in cans that size probably knew something.

I sat back down. My shirt was stuck to my back and I felt a little sick.

I knew the list. Everybody who has ever looked up their own symptoms at two in the morning knows the list. Chest pain, the left arm, sweating, nausea. I'd read that page during the last crunch and closed the tab because it made me nervous, and then I'd felt better by morning and forgotten about it.

I picked up my phone.

I thought about calling an ambulance. I pictured two paramedics coming up in the elevator and finding a thirty-two-year-old man alone in an open-plan office, with energy drink cans lined up on the desk and an anime game paused on the second screen. Then I pictured the bill. I opened my contacts instead, as if somebody in there was going to tell me what to do.

My manager was at the top because I'd called him most recently. Then my dentist. Then a delivery driver whose number I'd saved two years ago. I'd tipped him far too much by accident, and he'd called to ask if I'd meant to, and I'd been so surprised that anybody would bother calling me that I saved his number. Below him were a few people from university I hadn't spoken to in years, my landlord, and a number labeled DO NOT ANSWER.

I scrolled through it twice and put the phone face down on the desk.

I decided to rest my head for five minutes. If it hadn't passed in five minutes, I'd call. That seemed reasonable. I folded my arms on the desk next to the keyboard and put my head down on them, facing the second monitor, where the game was sitting on the results screen of the last stage I'd cleared. The menu music was the same loop I'd heard maybe four hundred times that week.

The edges of the screen went soft. My heartbeat was loud in my ears, and then it went strange, too fast and then too slow, as if it had lost count. The timer in the corner of the event screen said one hour and fifty-two minutes. I remember thinking that I still hadn't claimed the milestone rewards, and that I'd do it in five minutes, once my arm felt better.

---

I woke up and I couldn't lift my head.

I tried, and my head stayed exactly where it was. My neck didn't seem to be connected to anything.

The light was wrong too. Everything was blurred, a bright gray smear above me with something dark turning slowly in the middle of it. It took me a long time to work out that the dark thing was a ceiling fan and the gray was a ceiling.

Then there was the noise. Somewhere close, a lot of people were crying, high and thin and angry, several voices at once. Further away, women were talking fast over the top of it.

They were speaking Mandarin. I knew that much. I knew maybe forty words of Mandarin, and most of them came from food delivery apps.

I tried to ask where I was. What came out of my mouth was a wet, furious wail. It joined all the other crying, and that was when I understood that one of the crying voices was mine and I couldn't make it stop.

My body was doing everything on its own. It cried and shook and kicked against some kind of cloth wrapped tight around it. My heart was going so fast I could feel it in my ears. If I'd had an adult body, I think I would have been screaming and running into walls.

My head stayed calm. It just started working, the same as it did when production went down at four in the morning and everyone else on the call was panicking. What did I know? I'd been at my desk. I'd had chest pain and a numb arm. I'd put my head down. Now I was here, wherever here was, and I couldn't lift my head or see properly or control my own voice, and the people around me spoke a language I didn't.

I went through the explanations one at a time. The first was a hospital, with me drugged and the crying coming from other patients. That one fell apart when a huge shape leaned over me and picked me up with one hand under my back and one under my head, as easily as picking up a shoe. The shape was a woman. Her face filled the whole world. She said something soft and bored and jiggled me, and then she laid me down somewhere else and went to work on the lower half of my body with a damp cloth, and that settled the question. I was a baby, lying in a row of other babies in a room that smelled of milk and bleach, and there was nothing I could do about any of it.

---

I spent the next few months on my back.

People who've never been a baby with an adult mind probably think it sounds peaceful. It was the most boring thing that has ever happened to me, and I had once sat through a four-hour compliance training on the correct way to name files.

My eyes got better slowly. The gray smear turned into a ceiling with a brown water stain shaped like a fat bird. The dark shape turned into a fan with one blade bent so that it clicked every time it came around. I counted the clicks. I gave up at eleven thousand.

The room was long, with metal cribs in two rows. I could see three other babies from mine if I turned my head, which by then I could. Women in blue smocks came and went. They fed us in batches, changed us in batches, and talked to each other the whole time about things I couldn't follow. The other babies called them nothing, because they were babies. In my head I called them the aunties, because that was the one word they used for each other that I recognized.

I had wanted more time my whole adult life. Now I had nothing but time, and I spent it learning the difference between the aunty who sang while she worked and the aunty who held the bottle at the wrong angle.

When I'd been there a few weeks, a different woman came into the room.

She was small and she walked very straight. She had short gray hair and glasses hanging on a cord around her neck, and the aunties stopped talking when she came in. She went down the row of cribs, looking at each baby for a few seconds, and when she got to mine she stopped. She had a piece of paper in her hand. She put her glasses on and looked from the paper to me and back again.

"Eros," she said.

She said it carefully, the way people read out a word in a language they don't speak. It was the first word I had understood since I woke up, and it was my name.

My body did its thing again. My arms jerked and I started to cry. The woman watched me for a moment without any expression at all, took her glasses off, and moved on to the next crib.

I lay there and thought about it for the rest of the day. Did they know who I was? Had somebody brought me here? Was it a coincidence? There are only so many names in the world, but Eros isn't a name you hear much, in any country, and I'd never heard of anyone in China being called it. I made lists of explanations and couldn't test any of them. I was a few weeks old. I couldn't even ask.

---

By two I understood most of what people said around me. Babies are built for languages. I'd tried to learn Spanish as an adult with an app and quit after a week, and now Mandarin was just going into me without my trying, the way water goes into a sponge.

By three I understood everything, and I talked like a three-year-old on purpose. I'd thought about it carefully. A three-year-old who corrected the aunties' grammar would get noticed, and in my experience being noticed by people in charge was usually bad. They might take me to a doctor. They might take me to a temple. I didn't know which would be worse, so I said "want rice" and "no" and "why" like everybody else.

The place was called the Willow Street Children's Home. It was in Hangzhou, in an old three-story building that had been a dormitory for a textile factory before the factory closed. It had peeling green paint on the stairs and a courtyard where we were allowed to play when it wasn't raining, which in Hangzhou wasn't often. Thirty-one children lived there. The number changed now and then, but it always seemed to come back to thirty-one. There was one stove in the kitchen and one cook, and the toilets were down the hall and froze solid every January.

The small woman with the glasses was Director Zhou. She ran the place, and she ran it the way some people run a ship. She kept the accounts by hand in a big green ledger, she knew every child's name and birthday, and as far as I could tell she had never smiled in her life. The aunties were scared of her. So were the older kids, and so were the officials who sometimes came from the city with clipboards.

When I was four I asked her about my name.

She was at her desk in the little office by the front door, writing in the ledger. I'd gone in to bring her a cup of tea from the kitchen, because the cook had asked me to, and I put it down and stood there.

"Director Zhou," I said. "Why is my name strange?"

She didn't look up. "Who says it's strange?"

"Everybody. One of the big boys says it sounds like a sneeze."

"That boy can't count to twenty." She wrote down another number. "You were left at the gate. March of ninety-one. You were wrapped in a blanket. There was a piece of paper with you, with one word on it in foreign letters. I asked a man who teaches English at the middle school. He said it was a name."

"So you used it."

"It's what was written. I don't change what's written." She turned the page. "Go and wash your hands. You've got ink on them."

I didn't have ink on them. I went and washed them anyway.

That night I lay in the bed I shared with a boy who kicked in his sleep and thought about it again. Somebody had left me at a gate in Hangzhou in March 1991 with my own name written on a piece of paper. My name from before. It was a strange enough thing that I couldn't stop turning it over, and there was nothing at all I could do about it at four years old. The next morning there was egg in the porridge, which almost never happened, and I stopped thinking about it for a while.

---

I worked out how the place worked the way I'd work out a new codebase. You watch what happens and you look for the rules nobody wrote down.

Couples came on some Saturdays. They talked to Director Zhou in her office and then walked through the rooms with her, and mostly they looked at the babies. The babies who were picked went away and didn't come back. Children over three got looked at less. By the time I was four nobody looked at me for long, and by five they mostly didn't look at all. I didn't mind that as much as I probably should have. I'd already had a whole life without parents. I knew how it went.

What bothered me was the other rule. Thirty-one children and a handful of adults meant the adults had no time for anybody who didn't need them. The loud kids got attention because they were trouble. The sick kids got attention because they were sick. The quiet kids, the ones who ate their rice and did what they were told, got nothing. Nobody was cruel about it. There just wasn't enough to go around.

I was a quiet kid, by design. If I kept being one, I'd be at Willow Street until I was old enough to be sent to a factory.

The fastest way out of a place like that is to be worth something to someone who can get you out. I thought about that for a few weeks. Then I stopped hiding, a little at a time.

I started small. I read the newspapers that were left in the office and let the aunties catch me doing it. I helped the younger kids with their characters in the evenings, and some of them started reading earlier than they were supposed to, and the aunties noticed that too. Then, when I was six, Director Zhou caught me doing her accounts.

She was at her desk with the ledger and an old calculator with sticky buttons. She muttered the numbers under her breath when she added them up. I was sweeping the hallway outside, and I'd been listening to her for about ten minutes. When she paused at the bottom of a column, I said the total.

She looked up.

I said it again.

She looked down at the column, added it on the calculator, and looked back at me. Then she read out another column, fast, eleven numbers with decimals, and I gave her that total too.

"Who taught you?" she asked.

"Nobody."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she put her glasses back on and said, "Finish the hallway."

The next morning there were pages of sums on her desk with my name on top, and the morning after that there were pages of reading. It went on for a week. She never told me if I'd done well. She marked everything in red and handed it back and gave me more. At the end of the week she took me by the hand and walked me twenty minutes across the district to a primary school, where the headmaster turned out to be a man she'd taught with years before. They talked in his office with the door closed while I sat outside on a bench. When they came out, he looked at me the way you'd look at a dog that had just said hello.

I started in the third grade the next Monday, at six. When I got back to the home that evening, I went to the kitchen to see if there was any rice left, and Director Zhou was there talking to the cook.

"Bowls," she said, without looking around.

"I go to school now," I said.

"Thirty-one children. One stove. Wash your bowl."

I washed my bowl. I washed everyone's bowls that night, because it was my turn on the list, and nobody on the list ever got skipped for being clever.

---

When I was seven I tried to make her rich.

I'd been thinking about it for a while. I remembered roughly what was going to happen to the world over the next thirty years, at least the parts that had to do with computers. I knew that the internet was about to change everything, and I knew that one of the biggest companies in the world was going to start in a small apartment somewhere in this very city in a year or two. I didn't remember the exact date or the address. I remembered enough.

I explained it to Director Zhou at breakfast. I told her that the home should put its savings, all of them, into a company that was going to sell things to people through computers.

She listened to the whole thing while she ate. When I was done, she put her spoon down.

"What's the company called?"

"It doesn't exist yet."

"Where is it?"

"I don't know yet."

She picked her spoon back up. "Finish your porridge."

---

In February 2000, a month before I turned nine, the news showed a disaster in Russia.

We had one television, an old set in the common room with a coat hanger stuck in the back where the antenna had been. In the evenings the older kids were allowed to watch it for an hour, and the news came on before the cartoons, so we sat through the news.

That night it was mostly footage from far away. A flat white landscape under a gray sky, and a column of black smoke going up from the horizon. Then a road with a checkpoint on it, soldiers in heavy coats, trucks with their headlights on in the middle of the day. The newsreader said there had been an incident in a remote area of northern Russia. The Russian government had closed the area. There were no figures yet on casualties. Experts suggested a chemical accident or an explosion at an industrial site.

Northern Russia, which meant Siberia, in the year 2000.

For about a minute I sat there on the floor of the common room with a bowl of sunflower seeds in my lap, and I thought about Honkai Impact.

The Second Eruption happened in Siberia in 2000. I'd read about it a hundred times. The Herrscher of the Void, and Cecilia Schariac, and everything that came after it for the Kaslana family. I remembered the cutscenes and the wiki pages and the arguments about the timeline. For about a minute I sat very still and looked at the smoke on the screen and my heart started to speed up.

Then I laughed out loud. One of the little ones turned around and stared at me.

I'd spent almost nine years in this world. I had been to school and the market and the dentist, and I'd stood in line for train tickets at the Lunar New Year with Director Zhou and two hundred thousand other people. Nobody had a sword. Nothing had come out of the sky. The worst monster I had ever seen was the dentist. Russia was a big country with a lot of factories, and things blew up.

"Who's on dishes?" the cook shouted from the kitchen.

It was me. I got up and went. When I turned on the kitchen tap it coughed out some brown water and then nothing, because the pipe had frozen again, and I stood there holding a pot and thought that if I'd really been reborn into a game, the plumbing would be better than this.

---

After that, things went fast, because I made them go fast.

I skipped the fourth grade and most of the fifth. I won a math competition for the district and then one for the city, and a man from a newspaper came to Willow Street to take my picture. Director Zhou let him take one photo in the courtyard and then sent him away because he was in the way of the laundry. I got a scholarship to a middle school on the other side of the city, the kind with a gate and a uniform, and I took two buses there every morning and two buses back.

I was always the youngest in every room, by three years and then by four and then by five. The older kids didn't know what to do with me. Some of them were nice in the way you're nice to a kid brother, and some of them weren't, and most of them just ignored me. I ate lunch alone a lot. I'd eaten lunch alone a lot in my first life too, and I was used to it. I read during lunch. It was fine.

A lot of it was cheating.

I had thirty-two years of a life in my head, and ten of them had been spent writing software. In 2003, at twelve, I wrote a little program for a school competition that sorted and searched data in a way that wouldn't be normal for another ten years. The judges thought I was a genius. I didn't correct them. In 2005 I wrote a paper on a way of handling network traffic that I'd learned at my second job, out of a textbook that hadn't been written yet. It got me noticed by a university.

None of this bothered me. I'd learned those things once already, the hard way, at three in the morning. The fact that I got to learn them early didn't make them less mine. And nobody else could do it, so it wasn't as if I was stealing a spot from anyone who could.

I took the university entrance exam in the summer of 2006 and got into the computer science program at the big university in Hangzhou. I started in September 2006, at fifteen.

The university gave me a single room in a graduate dormitory because nobody knew what else to do with a fifteen-year-old. My first week, three different students asked me if I was someone's little brother who'd gotten lost. One of the professors asked me to fetch him a coffee before he realized I was in his class.

The only one who treated me like a normal student was Professor Han.

He was a heavy, soft-spoken man in his fifties who wore a sweater vest in every season and pushed his glasses up his nose with one knuckle. He taught the advanced algorithms course, and after my first assignment he asked me to stay behind.

"Well," he said, when the room was empty. He had my assignment on the desk in front of him and he was tapping it with his pen. "Perhaps you could explain to me where you learned this."

"Books," I said.

"Which books?"

I named a few real ones. He wrote them down. He didn't believe me, and he could tell that I could tell, and after a moment he put the pen down and sighed.

"Well," he said again. "Perhaps it doesn't matter. Would you like me to be your advisor?"

I said yes. He was the first adult since Director Zhou who looked at me and saw somebody to teach instead of something to show off, and he never asked me about the books again.

---

I went back to Willow Street every Sunday.

It wasn't really a decision. I just went, the same way I'd have gone home if I'd had a home. I took the bus across the city and spent the afternoon there. I helped the little ones with their homework, fixed whatever had broken that week, and ate dinner at the long table with everyone else. Director Zhou would ask for my grades and read every line of my report card while I stood there, then hand it back without a word. Then she'd point at the kitchen.

"I'm at university," I said once.

"Thirty-four children," she said. The number had gone up. "One stove."

So I washed the bowls.

In March 2007, a few days after my sixteenth birthday, one of the aunties called my dormitory phone and said Director Zhou was in the hospital.

She'd had a stroke at her desk. The cook had found her in the morning, slumped over the ledger with her glasses still on the cord around her neck. By the time I got to the hospital she was in a ward with five other old women, behind a curtain that didn't close all the way. The right side of her face had dropped. Her right hand lay on the blanket like it belonged to someone else.

She saw me and her eyes moved. She tried to say something and it came out thick and wrong, and I could see her hear it and hate it.

"Don't talk," I said. "It's fine. You don't have to talk."

She looked at me as if I'd said something stupid, which I suppose I had. Then she moved her left hand, slowly, and tapped the side of the bed twice. It took me a second to understand. Her handbag was on the chair. I picked it up and held it out, and she tapped it.

Inside, on top of her wallet and her reading glasses case and a folded newspaper, there was an envelope. It was old and soft at the corners. On the front, in her small, neat handwriting, it said Eros.

I opened it. Inside was a scrap of paper, yellow and brittle, folded once. When I unfolded it, there was one word on it in dark ink, in Latin letters, written by someone with steady handwriting who'd done a lot of writing in their life.

Eros.

I looked at it for a long time. When I looked back up, Director Zhou was watching me. Her face didn't do anything. I don't think it could have, even if she'd wanted it to.

"You kept it," I said.

She closed her eyes.

A nurse came and moved me out from behind the curtain because it was time for something, and I sat on a plastic chair in the corridor for three hours with the envelope in my lap. Then visiting hours ended and they sent me home.

She died four days later, in the night.

Four people came to the funeral. Me, the cook, a new director the city had sent over who had met her twice, and an old man who said she'd taught him math in 1962 and that she'd been the worst teacher he ever had and the only one he remembered. Afterward the new director stood in Director Zhou's office looking at the ledger and the files and the stacks of forms and visibly had no idea where to start. I sat down at her desk and did the paperwork for her death, because I was the only person there who knew how she filed things.

When I left that evening, I took the note out of the envelope and put it in my wallet, behind my student card, where it wouldn't get bent.

---

By the time I was seventeen I was bored with work. I was taking graduate courses and helping Professor Han with his research and writing code for a couple of companies on the side for money, and none of it was hard. I'd done all of it before. What I wanted was a problem I didn't already know the answer to.

So in the summer of 2008 I started building something stupid.

The idea was simple. A lot of data was public, if you knew where to look. Earthquake monitoring stations put their readings online. Weather satellites released their images, a few hours late. Power companies announced outages. Airlines posted delays and reroutes. Some cities even put out daily figures for ambulance calls. Nobody looked at all of those things together, because why would you? I wanted to see if I could find disasters before the news did, just by watching all the numbers at once and waiting for them to jump.

The first version crashed my laptop. The second version filled up my hard drive in three days. The third version, which I saved in a folder called weird_stuff_v3 because I couldn't think of a better name, ran on a secondhand server I kept under my desk, and it worked.

It found earthquakes. It found a chemical fire in Tianjin forty minutes before the first news report. It found a power plant failure in Brazil. That was all fun, and I was pleased with myself for about two weeks.

Then it started finding things it shouldn't.

There were events where everything spiked at once. A little seismic jolt with a strange shape to it, too sharp, like nothing natural I could find in any of the textbooks. A patch of satellite imagery where the clouds did something wrong, pulling away from a single point in a ring. Radio interference across a whole region. Power going down in a neat circle. Flights rerouted. Ambulance calls jumping in the nearest city. And then, a day or two later, a short news report about a gas leak, or a chemical fire, or a military exercise, or nothing at all.

I went back through as much old data as I could find. There were dozens of them, going back as far as the records went. Turkey, Peru, Canada, India, northern Japan, the middle of Australia. They all had the same signature, that same sharp little shape in the seismic readings. And when I lined them up by size, the biggest by a long way was in northern Russia, in 2000.

I sat in my room at one in the morning and looked at that dot on the map for a long time.

For the second time in my life, I wondered about Honkai Impact.

It was a stupid thought, and I knew it was a stupid thought, but I had it, so I tested it. I searched for Schicksal. I got a German dictionary entry, which told me it meant "fate," and the website of a metal band from Leipzig. I searched for Honkai and got nothing at all, which made sense, because it was a word somebody had made up for a phone game on a different planet. I searched for Kaslana and got a few people's family trees.

Honkai Impact had giant monsters walking through city centers and girls with swords fighting them on top of skyscrapers. My world had traffic.

My real guess was weapons testing. Every big country has a desert it doesn't talk about. Whatever it was, it was something that governments had spent a lot of money and effort keeping quiet, and I had found it with a secondhand server and public data, which I found very funny.

---

I showed it to Professor Han in January.

He sat at his desk with my laptop in front of him and scrolled through the map for a long time without saying anything. He clicked on Turkey. He clicked on Peru. He clicked on the big dot in Russia and read the numbers and then sat back and pushed his glasses up with his knuckle.

His pen was tapping on the desk.

"Well," he said. "Perhaps this is something to leave alone."

"It's all public data," I said.

"Yes. That's what worries me." He shut the laptop gently and pushed it back across the desk toward me. "Some data is missing because someone wanted it missing. Those people have phone numbers."

"So it's real."

"I didn't say that. I said leave it alone." He looked at me over his glasses. "You have a very long, very good career ahead of you, Eros. I'd like you to have it."

I said I'd think about it, and I meant it. I thought about it for about two months.

In March I turned eighteen. A week later, at two in the morning, I cleaned up the code, wrote a short paper explaining the method with the maps attached, and uploaded it to a preprint server under the title "Correlated Multi-Source Anomalies in Public Geophysical Data, 1990 to 2008." I listed myself as the only author so that none of it would land on Professor Han.

I wasn't worried. I'd been right about everything for eighteen years. I'd been right about the internet and about software and about which competitions to enter and which professors to pick. I was sure that the worst that could happen was a stern email from somebody important. Then I went to sleep.

---

The next evening I came back from the library at seven, and my door was unlocked.

I always locked it. I stood in the corridor with my key in my hand for a second, and then I pushed the door open.

There was a man sitting at my desk.

He was middle-aged and medium-sized, in a gray suit, with rimless glasses and short hair going gray at the sides. He had my laptop open in front of him and he was reading something on it, and he didn't look up when I came in. He just raised one finger, politely, as if I'd walked into a meeting that was almost over.

There was a woman too. She was leaning against the wall by my bookshelf with her arms folded. She was young, in her twenties, Japanese-looking, with a short black bob and a hoodie with the hood down. She was chewing gum. At her feet was a long black hard case, the kind people carry musical instruments in, except no instrument I knew of was that shape.

The server under my desk was dark. All its little lights were off.

My heart started going hard enough that I could feel it in my throat. My hands went cold. I'm not brave, and I've never been in a fight in either of my lives, and I could feel my legs wanting very badly to turn around and run.

But my head went clear the way it does, and I noticed things. Four steps back to the corridor. The stairs to the left, two flights down to the front door. My room was on the third floor, so the window was no good. When I'd come up the path ten minutes ago there had been a van parked across from the building with its engine running and its exhaust smoking in the cold, and I'd thought nothing of it. The woman hadn't moved, but she'd stopped chewing. The man still hadn't looked at me. He was very calm, and he was calm the way people are when they don't need to hurry because they've already decided what's going to happen.

I took out my phone and opened my browser. The preprint server gave me a page that said the paper had been withdrawn. My server's address didn't load.

"Please," the man said in Mandarin. He spoke carefully, with an accent, every tone exactly where it should be. "Sit. It's your room."

There was only one chair and he was in it. I sat on the edge of my bed.

He finished reading, closed the laptop, and took a folder out of a leather bag at his feet. He opened it on his knee.

"Eros," he read. "No surname. Born March 1991, in Hangzhou. Raised at the Willow Street Children's Home. Guardian, Zhou Lan, deceased 2007. No other family." He turned a page. "University at fifteen. Very impressive results. Very good references." He closed the folder and looked at me for the first time. His eyes were gray and polite. "My name is Brenner. This is my colleague, Miss Kudo."

The woman lifted two fingers off her arm in a little wave.

"How did you find us?" Brenner asked.

"Who's us?"

"The people whose data is in your paper."

They'd already read the code. It was sitting on the laptop he'd just closed. There was nothing to gain by playing dumb about something they could check in ten minutes.

So I told him. I explained the sources and how the program put them together and what it looked for. I told him about the strange shape in the seismic readings and how I'd matched it across events. He listened without interrupting, and now and then he nodded, as though I was confirming something he'd already worked out.

When I finished, he asked, "Why did you look at Russia first?"

"I didn't. It was just the biggest."

"Yes," he said. "It was." He sat for a moment, then reached into his jacket and took out a plastic ID card on a clip. He held it up so I could see it. There was a photo of him, a lot of small print in German and English, and in the corner, an emblem.

It was a cross, sharper than a church cross, with lines that flared at the ends, and I had seen it ten thousand times on loading screens and menu backgrounds and the shoulders of uniforms.

"What you found has a name," Brenner said. "We call it Honkai."

I went still.

Everything arrived at once. The cross. The smoke on the common room television, and the minute I'd spent sitting on the floor with sunflower seeds in my lap, and the joke about the plumbing. The dictionary entry that said Schicksal meant fate. A disaster in northern Russia in 2000 with no casualty figures, because there was nobody left to count them. Turkey and Peru and India and dozens of dots on my map, every one of them a place where something had come out of nowhere and killed people, and someone had cleaned up after it and called it a gas leak.

I was in Honkai Impact. I'd been in it for eighteen years, washing bowls, and somewhere in this world, right now, there were Valkyries. There were Herrschers. There was Otto Apocalypse, and Kevin Kaslana, and the Sea of Quanta, and the Imaginary Tree, and every single thing I had spent my last life reading about at two in the morning instead of sleeping. It was all real, and a man from Schicksal was sitting in my desk chair holding up his ID card like a parking permit.

I wanted to stand up and shout. I wanted to laugh until I couldn't breathe. I wanted to grab him by his gray lapels and ask him a hundred questions, starting with what year the Third Eruption was going to be and ending with whether I could see a Divine Key.

"Honkai," I said, and frowned a little. "I've never heard of it."

"No," Brenner said. "You wouldn't have."

Miss Kudo made a small noise through her nose that might have been a laugh.

Brenner put the card away. "What I'm going to say next, I'd rather say honestly. I'd rather not lie to you. It makes the paperwork longer." He folded his hands on top of the folder. "The organization I work for would like to offer you a position. You'd be well paid. You'd work on problems that I think you'd find very interesting, with resources you won't find anywhere else. Your university will be told that you've accepted a scholarship to study abroad. There is another option. Most people don't need to hear it, so I'd prefer..."

"Yes," I said.

He stopped.

"Yes," I said again. "I'll take the job."

Miss Kudo laughed out loud this time. "Most people ask questions first, genius."

"I've got a lot of questions," I said. "I'll ask them on the plane." I thought about it. "Actually, one now. Is there a salary?"

Brenner looked at me for a second longer than he needed to. Then he said, "There is a salary."

"Then yes."

He nodded slowly and took a pen from his inside pocket and wrote something in the folder. I would have given a lot to see what it was. When he was done, he capped the pen and looked up.

"Is there anyone you'd like to call before we leave?"

I thought about it, properly. I went through it the way I'd gone through my contacts on the last night of my first life. Professor Han would hear I'd gone abroad, and he'd worry, and then he'd be relieved, and he'd probably guess more than he'd ever say. The aunties at Willow Street wouldn't notice for a few Sundays. There was nobody else.

"No," I said.

Brenner wrote that down too.

"Pack one bag," he said, standing up. "Clothes, anything personal. The laptop stays with us, and so does the server. We'll take care of the rest."

I packed one bag. It didn't take long. I didn't own much, and most of what I did own was the laptop and the server. I checked my wallet while my back was to them. My student card was there, and behind it, folded once, the note.

Miss Kudo picked up her case with one hand as though it weighed nothing, and the three of us went down the stairs to the van.

---

The van took us to a small airport outside the city that I hadn't known existed. There was a private jet waiting on the runway with its stairs down, white with no markings at all. Inside it had eight leather seats and a little galley and carpet so thick my shoes sank into it.

All the window shutters were pulled down. When I reached for the one next to my seat, Miss Kudo said, "Leave it," without opening her eyes.

I left it. That told me more than they probably meant it to. If they didn't want me to see where we were going, then where we were going was somewhere they didn't want people to know about, and I could guess roughly how far by how long we flew.

Brenner sat at the front with his folder and a peppermint and didn't speak to anyone. Miss Kudo took the seat across the aisle from me, put her hard case on the floor between her feet, and fell asleep before the plane had finished taxiing. I'd never seen anyone fall asleep that fast. One second she was chewing gum, and the next her head was against the wall and her mouth was slightly open.

She woke up somewhere over the sea, or what I assumed was the sea, and stretched and looked at me.

"You're really eighteen?"

"Since last week."

"Huh." She found a new piece of gum in her hoodie pocket and unwrapped it. "You look about fifteen."

"I get that a lot."

She chewed for a while. I looked at the case between her feet. It was about a meter long, black, with metal corners and two latches, and she'd picked it up off my floor as if it were a lunchbox.

"What's in the case?" I asked.

"Lunch."

"It's a big lunch."

"I'm a big eater." She grinned and put her feet up on the case.

I was quiet for a while after that, because my brain was working very hard. She worked for Schicksal and carried a hard case the size of a rifle and had walked into a stranger's room without a hint of nerves. She lifted that case one-handed. She was in her twenties and dressed like a student. Schicksal's field people, the ones who did the fighting, were almost all young women.

She was a Valkyrie. I was sitting across the aisle from an actual Valkyrie, and I couldn't say a single word about it.

It was the hardest acting job of my life. I kept wanting to ask her what rank she was, what her battlesuit was, whether she had stigmata and from whom. My mouth was full of questions that nobody who'd never heard the word "Honkai" two hours ago would know how to ask. I could feel them building up behind my teeth.

In the end, she told me herself, because she was bored.

"You're going to see a lot of girls carrying cases like this," she said. "So you know, we're called Valkyries. Don't make it weird."

"Like the opera?"

She stared at me. "What opera?"

"There's a German opera..."

"No. Not like the opera." She rolled her eyes. "Valkyries are the ones who fight. What you found on your little map? Those were places where Honkai broke out, and then somebody like me went in and dealt with it. Well. Not like me." She tapped her own chest. "D-rank. Logistics. I'm medical and transport. I drive the trucks and fly the helicopters and stitch people up when they come back. And I pick up geniuses from their dorm rooms when Brenner needs an escort and everyone better is busy."

"D-rank," I said. "Is that bad?"

"It's the bottom," she said cheerfully. "D and C are support. B and up are the ones who go in first. A-ranks are the aces. S-ranks..." She waved a hand. "S-ranks are basically a story they tell recruits. There's maybe one or two in the whole world at a time, and some years there's none. You'll never meet one. If you ever do, be polite."

"What do the ones who go in first fight?"

"Things you don't want to see." She closed her eyes again and slid down in her seat. "You'll see the files. Wake me up when we land."

I sat there with my hands in my lap. I'd come very close, a few seconds earlier, to asking her whether Logistics Valkyries were issued the standard stigma sets or customized ones. I'd actually had my mouth open. If I'd said it, Brenner would have turned around in his seat at the front of the plane and looked at me with his polite gray eyes.

---

We landed at night. The plane taxied straight into a hangar, and the hangar doors closed behind us before they let the stairs down, so I never saw the sky. For the few seconds before the doors shut, the air coming in smelled of salt and seaweed. A car took us down a ramp into an underground garage. A lift took us up. When the doors opened, there was a long corridor with a white floor and white walls and soft lights in the ceiling, and it smelled of floor cleaner and air conditioning, like every corporate office I'd ever worked in.

Halfway down the corridor there was a portrait.

It was big, in a gold frame, lit from above. It showed a man in white and gold robes, standing with one hand resting on a book. He had long pale blond hair and a calm, handsome face, and he was smiling slightly, like a man listening to someone tell him something he already knew. The brass plate under it said OTTO APOCALYPSE, BISHOP OF SCHICKSAL.

I slowed down without meaning to.

The fan in me was thrilled. This was the man. The man who'd been running Schicksal for nearly five hundred years, who had schemed his way through the whole history of the series, whose name came up in half the arguments I'd ever had on the forums. I knew the color of his eyes. I knew things about him that the people in this building would never know.

And because I knew those things, I knew the other part too. Otto collected interesting people. He had labs. He had experiments. He had, if I remembered right, clones growing in tanks somewhere, and he did what he wanted with the people who caught his attention, and nobody in all those centuries had stopped him.

I decided right there in the corridor that I was going to be the most boring employee Schicksal had ever hired.

"Keep up," Miss Kudo said over her shoulder.

I kept up.

---

The intake was in a medical room on the next floor. Miss Kudo put her case in a locker, washed her hands, and pulled on a pair of gloves, and suddenly she wasn't a bored escort anymore. She took my blood pressure and my temperature, looked in my ears and eyes and throat, weighed me, measured me, and filled out a form on a clipboard in fast, messy handwriting.

"Last one," she said, wheeling a machine over from the corner. It looked like a blood pressure monitor that someone had crossed with a car battery. A thick cable ran from it to a metal cuff. "Give me your arm."

"What is it?"

"Honkai Adaptability. It tells us how much Honkai energy your body can handle." She snapped the cuff around my forearm. It was cold. "It's going to sting a bit."

It stung more than a bit. Something like a needle went into the inside of my arm, and then a warmth spread up from it to my elbow, then to my shoulder, an odd buzzing heat as though my arm had fallen asleep from the inside. The machine hummed. A little screen on the front of it showed a row of letters, and a light moved along them from right to left and stopped.

F.

Miss Kudo glanced at it and unclipped the cuff. "Normal for a guy," she said. "Don't take it personally."

I took it personally. I'd known it was coming. I knew the lore. Men barely tolerated Honkai energy, and Valkyries were all women. The rare men who could handle it became Schicksal Knights instead, people like Siegfried Kaslana, with something special in their blood. I was an orphan software engineer. I'd known all that. Somewhere underneath it, though, I'd been hoping anyway, the way every fan of every story secretly hopes they'd be the exception. A little part of me had expected the light to keep moving down the row.

"Can it change?" I asked. "The grade?"

She shrugged and peeled off her gloves. "For girls, a bit, with training. For guys..." She dropped the gloves in the bin. "I've never seen it. Doesn't matter for you, anyway. You're not going to be anywhere near the stuff."

A man from administration came in with a laminated badge. It had my photo on it, taken an hour ago in the hangar, with my hair sticking up on one side. Under the photo it said EROS, and under that, SOFTWARE ENGINEER, COMBAT SIMULATION AND BATTLESUIT SYSTEMS.

He took me to a room on a residential floor. It had a bed, a desk, a wardrobe, a small bathroom, and a window that looked out onto a concrete wall about two meters away, lit by a lamp. I stood at the window for a minute looking at the wall. Then I lay down on the bed with my shoes still on and stared at the ceiling until I fell asleep.

---

The next morning, the man from administration took me to the eleventh floor.

It was an open-plan office, like every office I'd ever worked in, with rows of desks and monitors and people hunched over keyboards, and that same smell of coffee and carpet. Nobody looked up when we walked through. At the far end was a glass-walled office with the blinds half down, and on the door was a printed sign that said DR. K. MORI, CHIEF ENGINEER. Under it, someone had stuck a yellow sticky note that said DO NOT KNOCK UNLESS ON FIRE.

The man from administration knocked.

"What," said a voice inside.

The office was a mess. There were three monitors on the desk, two showing code and the third showing a crash log that was scrolling slowly. There were papers everywhere, binders stacked on the floor, and on the corner of the desk, a pyramid of empty canned coffee, four levels high, built with real care. Against the back wall was a folding cot with a blanket and a pillow on it, and the pillow still had a dent in it.

The man behind the desk was in his late forties and looked as though he'd been awake since some time last week. His shirt was untucked on one side, his lab coat had a coffee stain down the front, and there was a pair of reading glasses pushed up into his hair that he clearly didn't know were there, because while we stood in the doorway he patted his shirt pocket looking for them.

"This is the new engineer," the man from administration said. "Eros."

Dr. Mori looked at me. He looked at my badge. He looked back at me.

"You're the weather data kid."

"Yes."

"You found us with weather data."

"And seismic data. And some other things."

"Fine." He reached down beside his desk without looking, came up with a binder about five centimeters thick, and dropped it on the desk in front of me with a thump. "Here's three years of bugs."

I looked at the binder. I looked at the cot behind him with the dent in the pillow. I looked at the coffee pyramid.

I knew that pyramid. I'd built one exactly like it in my last office, with energy drink cans, and on the last night of my first life it had been sitting next to my keyboard while my left arm went numb. I'd died at a desk like this one. Now I'd been reborn, grown up all over again, and been recruited by a secret organization that fought monsters from the end of the world, and the first thing it had handed me was the same job that had killed me.

"Is something funny?" Dr. Mori asked.

"No, sir."

"Don't call me sir. Sit there." He pointed at a chair that had papers on it. I moved the papers and sat. "You know what we do here?"

"No." This was true. I didn't know anything about this specific team.

"Two things." He held up one finger. "Training simulations. Valkyries train in virtual environments before we send them anywhere real. We build the environments, the enemies, the physics, all of it. If our simulation says a thing moves this fast and hits this hard, and then the real thing moves faster and hits harder, a girl dies." He held up a second finger. "Battlesuit firmware. Battlesuits are the combat suits Valkyries wear. They're full of software. If the software crashes in the middle of a fight, the suit locks up." He didn't say what happened then. He didn't need to. "Questions?"

"Lots. Later."

"Good answer." He spun one of his monitors around to face me. "You'll get a workstation out there. Until then, look at this."

It was a file system, a big tree of folders, project names I didn't recognize, version numbers, dates. He showed me where the simulation code lived and where the firmware lived and how the build system worked, which was badly, and talked very fast the whole time, naming parts of the code by nicknames as though I'd been there for years. One module was "the Swamp." Another was "Grandma." He didn't explain either.

While he was scrolling, I saw a folder near the top of the tree with a little red lock icon on it. The name of the folder was APOCALYPSE, T.

I coughed. I coughed quite hard, actually, and had to look at the floor for a second.

"You sick?" Dr. Mori asked.

"Dry air."

"Drink water. Nobody here drinks enough water." He scrolled past the folder without looking at it. Theresa Apocalypse's files were sitting in a locked folder on my new boss's computer, and he had scrolled past them as if they were somebody's tax returns. "Come on. You need to be registered on the core or you can't run anything."

---

The core was in the basement, three security doors down.

The room was cold and loud with fans, and it was full of server racks, rows and rows of them, blinking. In the middle of the room, behind a waist-high railing, stood a cylinder about two meters tall, made of dark metal and glass. Inside the glass there was something like light, but it moved too slowly to be light. It drifted and folded over on itself. It was mostly white with something underneath that wasn't quite a color, and when I looked straight at it, my eyes wanted to slide away.

The whole room hummed. The hum was low enough that I felt it in my chest more than I heard it.

"Computing core," Dr. Mori said. "All the heavy simulation runs on this. It's faster than anything you've ever used by a stupid amount." He walked over to a panel on the railing, a flat black surface with a handprint outlined on it. "Runs on Honkai energy."

I stared at it.

"Don't lick it," he said.

"I wasn't going to."

"The last kid did." He tapped the panel and it lit up. "Hand here. It reads your biometrics and gives you access. Takes a few seconds."

I put my hand on the panel.

For a moment there was nothing, just cool glass under my palm. Then a jolt went up my arm, a hard, bright shock from my palm to my shoulder, and my teeth clicked together. The light in the cylinder flickered, or I thought it did. Then it was cool glass again, and a line of green text on the panel said ACCESS GRANTED. I left my hand there a second longer than I needed to, and nothing else happened.

"You okay?" Dr. Mori said. He was looking at his own tablet, not at me. "It gave you a shock?"

"Static," I said.

"Does that." He tapped something. "Okay, you're registered. Welcome to Schicksal."

---

I spent the rest of that day at a workstation by the window, or what would have been a window if there'd been anything outside it but more concrete. I got a login. I opened the binder and read bug reports. Some of them were funny, in the way bug reports are funny. One said the simulated Honkai beasts sometimes walked through walls on Tuesdays and nobody knew why. Some of them weren't funny at all. One, about a firmware bug in the joint actuators of a battlesuit, had been closed eight months ago with a one-line note at the bottom that said Field incident, see report 2008-117. Subject deceased.

At eight Dr. Mori came out of his office, said "Go home," and went back in and lay down on the cot.

I went back to my room. I locked the door. I sat on the end of the bed and looked at the concrete wall outside the window for a while.

I lay back on the bed with my shoes on and did the math.

The Third Eruption happened in Nagazora in February 2014. I'd watched it play out a dozen times, in the opening chapters and in the flashbacks and in the fan videos that cut it all together with dramatic music. A whole city, and the people in it, and a girl in a school uniform standing in the middle of it with lightning coming off her. It was March 2009. That gave me a little under five years.

I didn't know where this building was. I'd smelled the sea in the hangar, and that was all I had. I didn't know how far it was from Nagazora, or whether anybody here would be sent there when it happened. I didn't know much about this world at all, really, except the parts that had been in a game. I knew how a lot of stories ended. I didn't know what anyone ate for lunch.

So I made a plan, the same way I'd plan a project with a deadline I couldn't move. Be boring, so that the man in the portrait downstairs never had a reason to think about me. Be useful, so that Schicksal had a reason to keep me fed and housed and close to the files. Learn everything I could about how this place really worked. And when February 2014 came, be somewhere far away from Nagazora, or at least be somewhere I could run from.

I got up and tore a blank page out of the back of Dr. Mori's binder and wrote it all down, every date I could remember from the game, in order. I wrote it as code, a scheduling function with the dates as constants and the events as function names that sounded like maintenance jobs. Anyone who found it would think I'd taken my work to bed on my first night, which was true enough. Then I folded it and put it in my wallet next to the note.

There was a kettle on the desk and a packet of instant noodles in the drawer that somebody had left behind. I made the noodles and ate them sitting on the bed, looking at the concrete wall outside the window, and then I set an alarm for six, because it was my first week.
